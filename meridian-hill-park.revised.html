Origins

A hill named for a line no one can see

Long before the fountains, this rise of land north of the White House was Nacotchtank territory, then a country estate, then a name tied to an invisible line running through the District.

In 1804 an obelisk was set on the bluff to mark the District's own meridian, once used to help keep local time. When John Porter built a mansion here in 1819, he named it Meridian Hill for the line running through the property. John Quincy Adams moved into that mansion when he left the White House in 1829, and Union troops later camped on the grounds during the Civil War.

The park itself began as a vision, not a plan: Mary Foote Henderson, a wealthy neighborhood landowner, campaigned from 1906 for a grand public garden on the hill. The federal government bought the land in 1910, and the 1902 McMillan Plan's call for formal European-style parks in Washington gave the project its design language.

The centerpiece

Thirteen basins, one long fall of water

The park splits into two distinct characters on one hillside: a French-style upper terrace, and an Italian cascade tumbling down to the street below.

The upper terrace runs 900 feet flat along the top of the hill, with a central lawn, side promenades, and a formal allée of linden trees — a layout borrowed from French garden tradition. At its northern end stands a bronze statue of Joan of Arc, facing out over the rest of the park.

The lower terrace is where the hill does the work. Peaslee modeled the design on the cascades at Italy's Villa Aldobrandini, sending water down thirteen graduated basins into a long reflecting pool — the longest cascading fountain in North America. The concrete itself was innovative for its time: exposed aggregate, mixed with pebbles and colored stone by artisan John Joseph Earley, so the walls read as textured stone rather than poured cement.

Monuments

Who stands watch in the park

Three figures anchor Meridian Hill's terraces, each installed decades apart and for very different reasons.

Joan of Arc, 1922

A gift from the women of France to the women of America, this bronze copy of Paul Dubois's original shows the saint mounted, sword raised, facing out from the upper terrace. In 1978 the sword itself was stolen — it wasn't replaced until 2011.

James Buchanan Memorial, 1930

The park's largest monument sits on the lower level: the fifteenth president, seated, flanked by allegorical figures of Law and Diplomacy. It was decades in the making — Congress authorized it in 1918, and Buchanan's niece funded much of it.

Dante and the drum circle

A bust of Dante Alighieri overlooks the western edge of the park. On Sunday afternoons the lower terrace has hosted an informal drum circle since the mid-1960s — one of the longest-running gatherings of its kind in the city.

Getting there and getting around

Location
Bounded by 15th, 16th, Euclid, and W Streets NW — Columbia Heights, about 1.5 miles north of the White House.
Hours
5 a.m.–midnight, May through October.
5 a.m.–9 p.m., November through April.
Nearest Metro
U Street or Columbia Heights, both a short walk away on the Green/Yellow lines.
Good to know
Dogs must stay on leash. The cascade fountain and reflecting pool are periodically drained for repair — check current conditions before a visit built around the water features.
Managed by
The National Park Service, as part of Rock Creek Park.
